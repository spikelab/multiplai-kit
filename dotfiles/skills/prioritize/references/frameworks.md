# The two frameworks

Both are old and both are usually applied too gently. What follows is the
version that actually changes a decision.

## Importance × urgency (Eisenhower)

The matrix isn't a sorting tool. Its only real job is to separate **important**
from **urgent**, because they feel identical in the moment and aren't.

|  | Urgent | Not urgent |
|---|---|---|
| **Important** | **Q1** — do | **Q2** — schedule |
| **Not important** | **Q3** — shrink, batch, or delegate | **Q4** — drop |

In this skill, `important` = plan-cli `importance: high`. `urgent` = due within
7 days, or overdue.

### The questions that make it bite

- **Q1 (important + urgent):** "When did this become urgent?" A Q1 item that
  was a Q2 item last month is evidence about the *system*, not the task. Note
  it once; a crowded Q1 is a diagnosis, not a moral failing.
- **Q2 (important, not urgent):** "What displaced this last week — and the week
  before?" This is the quadrant that decides whether the year goes well, and
  the one with no deadline forcing it. `starved: true` means 14+ days sitting.
  The usual fix is a self-imposed due date, which converts it to Q1 on purpose.
- **Q3 (urgent, not important):** "Whose deadline is this?" If the answer is
  someone else's and the work is small, batch it into a sand block. If it's
  large and still not important, that's the conversation — it's consuming a
  week on someone else's priorities.
- **Q4 (neither):** "If this is still here in a month, will anything be worse?"
  If no, it belongs out of the backlog. A backlog nobody trusts stops being a
  source of truth, which is worse than a shorter backlog.

### Where it fails

The matrix has nothing to say about **sequencing** (what unblocks what),
**energy** (the hard thing needs the good hours), or **compounding** (the thing
that makes the next five things faster). When one of those is the real
constraint, name it and override the quadrant — the matrix loses that argument.

## Rocks / pebbles / sand

The jar parable: put the sand in first and the rocks never fit. It's about
**order of placement**, not a taxonomy of tasks.

- **Rock** — one per day. The protected block on work that only matters if it
  gets uninterrupted time. Becomes the `[now]` focus.
- **Pebbles** — 2–3 real pieces of work that fit around the rock.
- **Sand** — ≤15-minute items that fill the gaps between meetings. plan-cli
  gives these a momentum bonus for a reason: they're how a fragmented day
  still produces something.

### The questions that make it bite

- "Is the rock actually a rock, or a pebble you're afraid of?" Fear inflates
  size. An unestimated task is often a rock *and* under-specified — chunk it.
- "Does the day physically fit?" Rock minutes + pebble minutes + meetings
  against real hours. If it doesn't fit, cut in the plan rather than discover
  it at 4pm.
- "What's the sand for the 10-minute gaps?" Without a named list, gaps go to
  email.

### Why one rock

Two rocks is a hedge, and hedged days end with two half-finished things. plan-cli
enforces this structurally — only one task can be `[now]`.

## How they compose

Eisenhower argues **what deserves rank**. Rocks/pebbles/sand decides **what
today can physically hold**. Ranking without shaping produces a correct list
nobody can execute; shaping without ranking produces a full day of the wrong
work.

Run them in that order, and let the day plan be honest about what it excludes.
